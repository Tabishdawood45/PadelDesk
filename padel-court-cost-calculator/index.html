What a padel court really costs in 2026

Headline "court prices" are misleading because the court structure is only part of the project. A complete build has four big line items:

  • Court system — steel frame, 10–12mm glass walls, mesh, turf and sand. This is the price most suppliers quote.
  • Groundwork — a reinforced concrete ring beam and base with drainage, designed for local wind loads. Often 35–45% of the whole budget and the item first-time buyers forget.
  • Lighting — LED floodlights if you want evening play (where most club revenue is).
  • Cover — a canopy or full building. In rainy markets like the UK this is what makes a court bookable year-round; it can cost more than the court itself.

How much does it cost to build a padel court?

The short answer: a single outdoor padel court costs roughly $25,000–$60,000 in the US, £45,000–£80,000 in the UK, or €30,000–€60,000 in France and most of Europe. But those are just the court-system-plus-installation figures. The total project cost — from bare ground to match-ready court — is usually higher, because the court kit is only one piece of the puzzle.

Court type affects cost more than you'd expect

Not all padel courts are built the same way. The three main configurations carry different price tags:

  • Classic courts ($23,000–$38,000) — steel posts with mesh walls and standard turf. Perfectly fine for recreational play and most club settings.
  • Panoramic courts ($35,000–$50,000) — one-piece glass walls on the back and parts of the sides, giving spectators a clear view. The most common choice for commercial clubs.
  • Full panoramic courts ($50,000–$75,000+) — glass walls all the way round, as used on the professional padel tours. Impressive, but the extra glass and precision assembly add cost.

Maintenance and running costs

The build price is not the end of the spending. Plan for these ongoing costs:

  • Annual maintenance: routine brushing, sand top-ups, hinge and hardware checks. Budget around £400–£500 per year (or $500–$700 / €400–€600) for basic upkeep.
  • Turf replacement: artificial turf lasts 5–10 years depending on usage and climate. Replacement runs €5,000–€10,000 (or $5,000–$13,500 / £4,000–£10,000) per court — start a sinking fund from day one.
  • LED bulb replacement: modern LEDs last 50,000+ hours, but expect some lamp or driver replacements after 7–10 years.
  • Glass and frame: tempered glass panels can crack from impacts or settling foundations. Inspect annually for chips, anchor movement, or unusual vibration.

With proper care, the overall court structure lasts 10–20 years before major renovation is needed. Budget builds with poor drainage or low-quality steel often fall below 12 years.

Five things that move your quote

  1. Ground conditions. Building on an old tennis court can save £10,000+ per court versus virgin ground that needs excavation and drainage.
  2. Wind and corrosion. UK guidance requires structures engineered for local wind loads and better corrosion protection than continental Europe — cheap imported courts can be a false economy.
  3. Multiple courts. Site work, access and planning are largely fixed costs, so the per-court price drops when you build two or more at once.
  4. Planning and noise. Court lighting and ball-strike noise are the common objections. Budget time (and sometimes acoustic fencing) for approval.
  5. Maintenance. Plan £1,500–£3,000 per court per year for cleaning and minor repairs, and a sinking fund for turf replacement every 5–10 years.

Is a court a good investment?

Demand is running ahead of supply in all three markets: Britain passed one million active players in 2026 with court occupancy around 85% at peak and roughly half of UK players reporting difficulty booking peak slots; France added more than 1,000 courts in a year and passed 5,000; the US crossed 1,000 courts with padel now in 37 states. That's why planning applications for UK padel courts in the first half of 2026 already more than doubled all of 2025. None of this guarantees returns — location, cover and operations decide — but the underlying demand is real.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a padel court cost?
A single outdoor padel court typically costs $25,000–$60,000 in the US, £45,000–£80,000 in the UK, or €30,000–€60,000 in Europe. These figures cover the court system installed — frame, glass, turf, and basic lighting. Add groundwork on a new site and the total can double. Indoor courts with a building structure cost considerably more. See the full breakdown above for component-by-component numbers.
How much does it cost to build a padel court from scratch?
Building from scratch — meaning on bare land with no existing hard surface — is the most expensive route. You'll need excavation, a reinforced concrete slab with proper drainage ($5,000–$35,000 alone), plus the court structure, turf and lighting on top. All-in, expect $40,000–$110,000 in the US or £60,000–£150,000+ in the UK for a single court, depending on soil conditions and location. Converting an existing tennis court or car park can cut groundwork costs by 50–70%.
Can I build a padel court at home?
If you have roughly 11 × 21 metres of accessible flat space, yes. Home builds in the UK typically run £25,000–£80,000 depending on kit quality and groundwork. If you already have a tennis court or similar hard surface, the groundwork saving is significant. Check our calculator with "existing base" selected, and confirm planning rules with your local authority before committing.
What is the cheapest way to build a padel court?
The cheapest path is a classic (non-panoramic) court on an existing hard surface — an old tennis court, a concrete yard or similar. With mesh walls instead of full glass, no canopy and basic lighting, you can build a playable court for around $23,000–$38,000 in the US or £25,000–£40,000 in the UK. The trade-off is aesthetics and spectator visibility, but the court plays the same.
Do I need planning permission for a padel court?
In the UK, outdoor padel courts almost always need planning permission — the glass walls, steel frame and floodlighting are permanent structures. Noise from ball strikes is a common objection, so budget time for a noise assessment if neighbours are within 30 metres. Some indoor conversions within existing commercial buildings may qualify under permitted development rights without a full application. In the US, requirements vary by state and municipality — check local zoning and building codes. In France and the EU, a permis de construire or similar local permit is typically required.
How long does it take to build a padel court?
The court installation itself takes days to a few weeks. What stretches projects to months is groundwork, planning approval and (for indoor builds) the building structure. As a rough timeline: 2–4 months for planning, 2–6 weeks for groundwork and base, and 1–2 weeks for court installation. Modular indoor buildings can go up in as little as 4 weeks once planning is approved.
How big is a padel court?
The playing area is 10 × 20 metres (roughly 33 × 66 feet). With the structure, clearance and access, plan for around 11 × 21 metres per court, plus circulation space if you're building multiple courts side by side.
How long does a padel court last?
With proper maintenance, the overall court structure lasts 10–20 years before major renovation. Artificial turf is the first component to wear — expect to replace it every 5–10 years at a cost of €5,000–€10,000 per court. Steel frames and tempered glass panels last much longer if properly maintained. Budget builds with weak drainage or low-grade steel tend to fall below 12 years.
Is building a padel court a good investment?
Demand is strong: UK planning applications for padel courts in January–July 2026 already more than doubled the full-year total for 2025. Court occupancy at established UK clubs runs around 85% at peak times. In Europe, France alone passed 5,000 courts, and the US crossed 1,000. That said, returns depend on location, whether you have cover for year-round play, and operations. A covered multi-court venue in a dense urban area has a very different payback from a single uncovered court in a small town.
Why is the US price range so wide?
The American market is young: courts and glass are mostly imported from Europe, freight costs vary, and state-level construction costs differ hugely. Florida, Texas and California have the most courts but also different labour rates and building codes. Few published benchmarks exist yet, so treat any single number sceptically and collect at least three quotes.
